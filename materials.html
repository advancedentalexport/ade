GUIDE 01 / MATERIALS & RESTORATIONS

Understanding common restorative materials.

Restorations differ in composition, construction, optical properties, and clinical indications. The notes below provide a starting point for discussing a case with a dental laboratory, rather than a universal material ranking.

01 / CERAMIC

Zirconia

Oxide ceramic

Zirconia crowns. Some restorations are milled as a single structure, while others use a zirconia framework with veneering porcelain.

Monolithic
A single zirconia structure, usually without external veneering porcelain.
Layered
A zirconia substructure with porcelain applied for additional characterisation.
Multilayer
A zirconia blank with built-in colour or translucency gradients; the term describes the blank, not necessarily a porcelain veneer.

Composition, connector dimensions, preparation, and occlusal conditions must be considered for each indication.

02 / GLASS CERAMIC

Lithium disilicate

Etchable glass ceramic

Lithium disilicate is a glass ceramic used for selected crowns, veneers, inlays, and onlays. Its optical properties and ability to be adhesively bonded can be relevant to case selection.

Preparation design, restoration thickness, substrate shade, and the manufacturer's instructions influence the final material choice and bonding protocol.

A specific product's approved indications should always be checked rather than assumed for all lithium-disilicate materials.

03 / METAL–CERAMIC

Porcelain-fused-to-metal

Metal substructure + porcelain

PFM restorations use a cast or milled metal framework covered with veneering porcelain. The metal provides a supporting structure while porcelain forms the visible tooth surface.

Framework design, alloy selection, available space, porcelain support, and occlusal contact locations affect laboratory planning. Veneering porcelain can chip under unfavourable conditions.

04 / PROSTHETIC DESIGN

Implant-supported restorations

A category, not a single material

Implant prosthetics may include crowns, bridges, or larger restorations attached to implants through compatible restorative components. The restoration can involve different materials and methods of retention.

The laboratory requires the implant system and connection details, platform information, restorative space, and other prescribed components before the prosthesis can be designed appropriately.

Compatibility and the relevant manufacturer's instructions are essential when specifying implant components.

FOR CASE PLANNING

Questions to settle before material selection

01

Where is the restoration?

Anterior and posterior regions may place different demands on appearance, clearance, and function.

02

What space is available?

Preparation geometry and restorative thickness must suit the intended material and design.

03

How will it be retained?

Adhesive bonding, conventional cementation, and implant-retained designs have different requirements.

04

What references are available?

Shade photos, opposing dentition, bite records, and the treatment plan help clarify the prescription.
